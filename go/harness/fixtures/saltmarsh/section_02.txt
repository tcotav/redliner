It took Ferris four days to open the case properly.

The brass was not brass. It was something that behaved like brass in
every way he could test and in no way he could not, and when he set his
loupe against it the metal showed a grain, like wood, running in a
direction that changed depending on which eye he closed.

Inside, the mechanism was wrong in a way that took him a long time to
name. There was no mainspring. There was no weight, no pendulum, no
escapement, nothing that stored force and released it in measured
amounts. There was only the turning thing, suspended in its glass, going
around at a rate his own pulse could not keep up with and then, an hour
later, at a rate a glacier would have found unhurried.

He wrote down the rates. He filled a notebook with them. On the ninth
day he understood that the rates were not random, and on the tenth day
he understood what they were counting, and he sat down on the floor of
his shop, in the sawdust, and he stayed there until it got dark.

The woman had said *the others are gone.* She had said it the way you
mention weather.

Ferris was fifty-one years old. He had repaired four thousand clocks. He
had never once, in all that time, been asked to repair something that
was already working exactly as it was meant to.
