The tide clock in Ferris Vane's shop had not worked in eleven years, and
he wound it every morning anyway.

He was winding it when the woman came in. She stood in the doorway with
the grey light behind her, and she did not close the door, so the cold
came in with her and moved along the floor to where he stood.

"You repair instruments," she said.

"I repair clocks."

"It is a kind of clock." She set the case on the counter between them.
It was longer than his forearm and bound in oiled canvas, and when she
unwrapped it he saw brass, and a great deal of glass, and something
inside the glass that turned slowly although nothing had wound it.

Ferris looked at it for a while. His hands stayed at his sides.

"Where did you get this."

"That isn't the question you want to ask."

She was right, and he disliked that she was right. He wanted to ask what
it measured. He wanted to ask why the turning thing inside it had
slowed, very slightly, when she set it down — as if it had noticed the
room, and found the room wanting.

"I can look at it," he said.

"You can keep it," she said. "I'm told you're the last one who knows
what it is." She was already turning. Her grey eyes caught the door
light. "The others are gone."

He did not ask which others. He did not ask her name. He stood in his
cold shop with the door open and let her go, and afterward he told
himself there had been nothing else to do, which was the first lie he
told about the instrument but not the last.
