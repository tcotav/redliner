Nineteen winters had passed since Renata Sowa last stood on the Bellwether town dock, and the dock had not improved. Three of the cleats were gone. The bait shop at the head of the pier had become a place that sold fudge and lake-glass jewelry, and it was closed for the season, its windows papered over with the sports pages of the Alpena News.

She had driven in from the airport in Traverse City, ninety-one miles, in a rental car that smelled of someone else's cigarettes. It was the ninth of October, a Thursday, and the wind came off Lake Huron with that particular flat cold she had spent two decades telling herself she did not miss.

Emil had died in March, eighty-one years old, in the hospice wing of the county hospital. The lawyer's letter had taken six weeks to find her in Portland. It said the shop on Water Street was hers and Kaja's, jointly, along with whatever was in it, and that the property taxes were four years in arrears.

The key still hung on a nail inside the meter box, which told her everything about how the town had changed, which was not much.

Inside, the shop smelled of varnish and cold iron and mouse. And there, on blocks in the center bay under a canvas tarp gone stiff with dust, was his boat, the Lyman. A 1962 hull, twenty-six feet of white oak and mahogany, half of it planked and half of it open ribs, exactly as her father had left it. He had been restoring that boat since she was fourteen. He had been restoring it, she supposed, the morning he died.

She stood with her hand flat on the cold curve of the stem and did not cry, and then she called a broker in Rogers City to ask what the building might bring.
