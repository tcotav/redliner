She meant to stay four days. She stayed through October.

She rented the room above the hardware store for two hundred a month and worked in the shop with the doors open and a kerosene heater going, and she learned, slowly and badly, what her hands had forgotten. Steam the plank. Clamp it wet. Bevel the land. Her father had taught her all of it before she was seventeen and she had thrown it away like a coat she'd outgrown.

Twenty-three years. That was the arithmetic she kept doing at two in the morning: she had been away twenty-three years, long enough that the girl at the marina counter who sold her bronze ring nails had not been born when Ren left town.

And her father had died two months short of his seventy-seventh birthday, which meant the number of years she had known him and the number of years she had avoided him were closer to even than she could stand to think about.

His boat sat behind the shop under a tarp of its own, the flat-bottomed duck skiff he had built in one weekend in 1988 out of marine ply and spite, after a man in Hammond Bay told him a boat like that couldn't be built in a weekend. Ren pulled the tarp off and found four inches of black water in it and a frog. She bailed it, because bailing was something she knew how to do without instruction.

On the twenty-second she found his pencil marks on the inside of the transom, a column of dates going back years, each one the day he'd finished some small piece: a rib, a plank, a scarf joint. The last entry was March fourth. Two lines below it, in the same soft pencil, he had written a fifth date that had not happened yet, the way a man writes a date he intends to keep.
