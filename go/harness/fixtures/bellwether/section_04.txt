They put her in the water on the fourteenth of November, which everyone agreed was the wrong month to launch anything.

The lake had come up all fall. That morning the water stood over the dock boards at the outer end, so that the last twenty feet of pier looked like a path laid across the surface, and men in waders swore cheerfully at the travel lift. Thirty-one feet of white oak and mahogany came off the slings and sat down on the water and did what old wooden boats do, which is leak, generously and without apology, until the planks took up.

Kaja came with a thermos and a folding chair and did not say anything about brokers.

Neither of them had been in the room at the end. The hospice nurse had phoned Kaja at six in the morning on the fifth of March to say that Emil had gone in the night, alone, sometime after the two o'clock check, and Kaja had driven the eleven miles in the dark and sat in the parking lot for forty minutes before she could make herself go in.

"He'd have hated November," Kaja said, watching the pumps run. "He'd have said we were showing off."

Ren said nothing for a long moment. Then she went below and came back with the shop ledger, open to the inside cover, where their father had written her Portland address in pencil and crossed it out and written it again, three times, three apartments, the last one current as of a year ago.

Neither of them cried where the other could see it. The boat kept leaking. By four o'clock it had almost stopped, and the two sisters sat on the dock in the failing light with their feet over the water, forty-one and forty-four years old, waiting for the wood to swell shut.
