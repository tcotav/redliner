Kaja came Saturday morning with two coffees and the expression of a woman who had rehearsed in the car.

She was heavier than Ren remembered, and gray at the temples, and she moved through the shop touching things the way you touch a dog you are not sure still knows you. She had lived eleven miles up the shore road the whole time, in the house on Vasser Creek with her husband and, until last spring, two enormous and identical brown dogs.

"You called a broker," she said.

"I called to ask a question."

"You called a broker." Kaja set one coffee on the bench, hard enough to slop it. "He was in that room four days. And you were on an airplane exactly zero times."

Ren did not answer, because the answer was true. Her sister had sat with him all four of those days, and had held his hand through the last four hours of it, and had sung him that stupid song about the pierogi while the machines counted him down.

What she thought of instead, absurdly, was the August she turned sixteen, the year the lake dropped so low that the whole harbor turned to a shallow gray plate. You could walk out forty feet past the end of the dock and not wet your knees. Kids rode bikes over the exposed bottom. Their father had stood at the shop door with a cup of coffee looking at his own dock pilings standing in air like something skinned, and he had laughed, because there was nothing else to do about the lake.

"He never once asked about you," Kaja said. "Not once, Ren. Every Christmas I sat across from him and he never said your name."

"Okay."

"Don't do that. Don't just say okay."

"Okay," Ren said, and Kaja laughed against her will, a wet, ugly, familiar sound, and then neither of them said anything for a while.
